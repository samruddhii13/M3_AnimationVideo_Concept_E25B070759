From Input to Output: Reading the Graph of a Function. Single-variable functions and graphs (M3 Calculus). Example: a ball thrown into the air. Samruddhi Meshram | E25B070759.

--- SCENE 1: HOOK ---
Imagine you throw a cricket ball straight up into the air.
How high is it after one second?
After two seconds?
And when does it come back down?
These questions have exact answers, and the tool that gives them is called a function.
A function is a rule that takes an input, here the time, and gives back exactly one output, here the height.
In this video, we will use this ball to understand three things: how inputs map to outputs, how to read a graph, and which key features tell the whole story.

--- SCENE 2: CONCEPT AND MAPPING ---
Let's make this precise.
A function assigns to each allowed input exactly one output.
Our input is the time, tee, in seconds.
Our output is the height, aitch of tee, in metres.
If the ball is thrown upward at twenty metres per second, and we ignore air resistance, physics gives the rule: aitch of tee equals twenty tee minus five tee squared.
Here we take gravity as ten metres per second squared, to keep the numbers clean.
Let's test the rule.
At tee equals one, we get twenty minus five, which is fifteen metres.
Here is the full mapping.
Zero goes to zero.
One goes to fifteen.
Two goes to twenty.
Three goes to fifteen.
And four goes to zero.
Notice that every input has exactly one arrow leaving it.
That is what makes it a function.
The set of allowed inputs is the domain, and the set of outputs the function actually produces is the range.

--- SCENE 3: THE GRAPH ---
Now let's turn this mapping into a graph.
Each input-output pair becomes a point: time goes on the horizontal axis, and height goes on the vertical axis.
Zero comma zero.
One comma fifteen.
Two comma twenty.
Three comma fifteen.
Four comma zero.
Join the points smoothly, and we get a curve called a parabola.
It rises, peaks, and falls symmetrically.
Be careful: this is not the path of the ball in space, because the ball moves straight up and down.
The graph shows height against time.
Notice also that at one second and at three seconds the height is the same, fifteen metres.
Two different inputs may share an output.
But one input can never have two outputs, because at any moment the ball has only one height.
That gives us the vertical line test: if every vertical line crosses a graph at most once, the graph shows a function.
A circle fails this test, because the line at ex equals zero crosses it twice.

--- SCENE 4: KEY FEATURES (WORKED EXAMPLE) ---
Now let's find the key features step by step.
First, the intercepts.
At tee equals zero, aitch is zero, so the graph starts at the origin.
To find when the ball lands, set aitch equal to zero: twenty tee minus five tee squared equals zero.
Factorising, five tee times, four minus tee, equals zero.
So tee is zero or tee is four.
The ball lands after four seconds.
Second, the highest point.
We can rewrite the rule as twenty minus five times, tee minus two, squared.
The squared term is never negative, so the height is largest when it is zero, at tee equals two.
There, aitch equals twenty.
The maximum height is twenty metres, after two seconds.
Third, the domain and range.
The formula accepts any real number, but the ball only flies from zero to four seconds, so the domain here is zero to four.
The height stays between zero and twenty, so the range is zero to twenty.
Finally, the behaviour.
From zero to two seconds the graph is increasing.
From two to four it is decreasing.
To measure how fast, look at the average change in height per second.
From zero to one: fifteen.
From one to two: five.
From two to three: minus five.
From three to four: minus fifteen.
A negative value means the ball is moving downward.
The climb slows down, stops at the peak, and the fall speeds up.

--- SCENE 5: INTERPRETATION ---
Now let's read the graph as a story.
The starting point says: the ball leaves your hand at height zero.
The peak says: for an instant, at two seconds, the ball is twenty metres up and stops rising.
The second intercept says: it lands after four seconds.
The symmetry says: it takes exactly as long to fall as to rise.
And the domain reminds us that this formula describes the ball only between zero and four seconds.
Before the throw, or after the landing, it says nothing useful.
[OPTIONAL] So every feature of the graph has a meaning in the real world.
That is the power of a graph: it shows a whole relationship at a glance.

--- SCENE 6: SUMMARY AND CLOSING FRAME ---
Let's recap three ideas.
One: a function gives each input exactly one output, and the vertical line test checks this on a graph.
Two: on a graph, each point is an input-output pair, with the input on the horizontal axis.
Three: the intercepts, the maximum, the domain and range, and where the graph rises or falls, tell the real-life story.
[OPTIONAL] Next time you see a graph, ask: what goes in, what comes out, and what does each feature mean?
